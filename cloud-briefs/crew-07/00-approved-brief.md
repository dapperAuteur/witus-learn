# Brief: The Sideline's Other Crew, Tracking, Replay and Injury Video (CREW-07)

**Status:** approved 2026-10-26 for build, as CREW-07. Public; held for a qualified reviewer who has worked one of
these jobs (a tracking or replay systems technician). Research tier 2: the course describes concussion and injury
review protocols, and must never teach medicine. Vendors and leagues (Hawk-Eye, the NFL, the Big Ten, the SEC) are
named as cited worked examples, the way CREW-05 named the NBA.

## 1. Description

A learner can explain what a stadium's tracking array and replay systems do for officials, broadcasters and the
people who keep athletes safe; who sits where on a game day (the booth, the carts, the truck, the remote desk);
which of those jobs need a medical licence and which do not; what the non-medical jobs ask for and pay; and how to
get hired into one. It is the job-shaped knowledge for the technician, operator and coordinator roles that vendors
and leagues post each season, taught for crew who already know a truck or an arena. It does not teach medicine,
and it does not teach a vendor's operator screens, which are proprietary and taught on the job.

## 2. Outline

1. **What a tracking array is:** cameras around the field, calibration, skeletal points per athlete plus the ball,
   racket or stick, 3D output at low latency; who uses the output (officials, broadcast, medical staff, sports
   scientists); the vendors (optical: Hawk-Eye, TRACAB, Second Spectrum; wearables: Catapult, KINEXON), each
   only as its own pages describe it.
2. **What review systems do for medical staff:** the NFL's booth (athletic trainer spotters, injury video review
   technicians, the medical timeout); World Rugby's head injury assessment and pitch-side video review; the line
   between who may diagnose and who may not. No identifiable athlete's injury is ever an example.
3. **The leagues and the rules:** replay and measurement in the NFL; optical tracking in the NBA; ball-strike
   technology and Statcast in MLB; line calling in tennis; goal-line and offside technology in football (soccer);
   the review system in cricket; replay in college conferences (Big Ten, SEC) with vendor technicians on site.
4. **The game day, hour by hour, from the postings:** arrival hours before kickoff; power, network and camera
   checks; calibration; synchronizing the medical monitors; radios; tagging plays at the spotters' direction;
   monitoring during the game; the report after; the remote desk that supports venue technicians.
5. **The jobs and the ladder:** systems technician (casual, by venue), match day technician and systems operator,
   injury video review operator (league-hired), college sports systems technician, live operations coordinator
   (remote desk), technology coordinator and analyst; what each asks, the pay posted, eligibility rules (for
   example, not a team's employee), and the season's timing.
6. **Getting hired:** where postings live (TeamWork Online, league boards, vendor careers sites, staffing
   providers), when they open, what to practice (signal flow, Windows and Wi-Fi troubleshooting, typing,
   documentation), the interview, and the freelance and contractor questions.
7. **Capstone:** write a game-day runbook for one role from the public record, with every step cited; or compare
   two vendors' systems from their own pages.

About 7 sections, 20 to 24 lessons, section quizzes and a final.

## 5. Rules for the build

- **Medicine (gate A6, confirmed by the publisher):** never teach diagnosis, assessment steps as procedure, or
  return-to-play decisions. Describe protocols only as the governing body's own public documents describe them, and
  say who is licensed to act. No identifiable athlete's injury, by name, date, game or description, is ever used.
- Teach only VERIFIED facts from `research/`. A job posting is a primary source for what that employer asked on that
  date; say so and date it. Pay figures are "as posted on <date>", never as current market rates.
- Operator screens, internal procedures and anything not public stay out.
- Vendor products are cited as worked examples, not endorsements.
