# The Sideline's Other Crew: What Review Systems Do for Medical Staff

**Researched:** October 26, 2026. Every source tagged V below was fetched and read that day. Search-result snippets were used only to find pages; nothing from a snippet is tagged V.

**Tags.** **V** = read in a page or PDF fetched on October 26, 2026, at the locator given. **I** = inferred from V facts, with the reasoning shown. **A** = assumed; never to be taught as fact.

**Scope.** Outline section 2 of the approved brief (`cloud-briefs/crew-07/00-approved-brief.md`): the NFL's injury video review system and its people; World Rugby's Head Injury Assessment (HIA) and pitch-side video review; one more body (the NCAA, with a short NHL note); the line between the licensed people who diagnose and clear and the non-medical people who run the video and the radios.

**Medicine rule (gate A6).** This pack records how review is organized and who is licensed to act. It does not record symptom lists, assessment steps, scoring cut-offs or return-to-play stages, even where a source prints them. Where a source counts its criteria, the count is recorded and the list is not. No athlete is named and no real injury is described; where a source ties a policy to an incident, only the policy and its date are kept.

## Boundary: who may diagnose, and who runs the video

In the sources' own words, the line sits here:

- **The NFL booth spotter is a certified athletic trainer, and still does not diagnose.** "The AT spotter does not diagnose any injuries or suggest treatment, but simply alerts the team's medical personnel to a potential injury and describes how the injury happened when possible" (National Football League [NFL], n.d.-a, section "On-field injury detection and intervention"). A second NFL page says spotters "are not tasked with injury diagnosis or treatment" (NFL, 2022b, paragraph on ATC spotters).
- **Diagnosis and the return decision belong to the club physician.** "The responsibility for the diagnosis of concussion and the decision to return a player to a game remain exclusively within the professional judgment of the Head Team Physician or the Club physician designated as responsible for the diagnosis and management of concussion" (NFL Head, Neck and Spine Committee, 2024, p. 5).
- **The video technician works at the spotter's direction.** "When the spotter spots a potential problem ... he or she tells the technician to tag and label the video" (NFL, n.d.-a, section "On-field injury detection and intervention").
- **The NFL's own posting for the technician asks for more than buttons.** It lists "Must be able to recognize and record player injuries" alongside running the system and troubleshooting Wi-Fi, audio and video (NFL, 2026, Summary and requirements list). No source fetched today defines what "recognize" means for this role or says in so many words that the operator may not diagnose. This is an open check, below.
- **World Rugby says plainly what non-medical staff may not do.** "Non-medical staff can alert their respective team medical staff that they have seen an incident that suggests an off-field HIA1 assessment or permanent removal. Non-medical staff cannot call for an off-field HIA1 assessment, this must be done by medical staff. Non-medical staff cannot overrule or question a call for an off-field HIA1 assessment requested by the on-field medical staff, MDD or referee" (World Rugby, 2022, pp. 18 to 19, Q21).
- **The NCAA puts the decision with team physicians and athletic trainers.** Its best-practices document affirms "the unchallengeable autonomous authority of primary athletics health care providers (team physicians and athletic trainers) to determine medical management and return-to-play decisions" (National Collegiate Athletic Association [NCAA] Sport Science Institute, n.d., p. 4).

**Reading it together (I).** Three tiers recur across the bodies: physicians who diagnose and clear (NFL club physician with the Unaffiliated Neurotrauma Consultant [UNC]; rugby Team Doctor and Match Day Doctor [MDD]; NCAA team physician); licensed health professionals who watch and can trigger an evaluation or stop play but do not diagnose in that role (NFL booth athletic trainer spotters; NHL central spotters); and non-medical crew who keep the video, the clips and the radios working and tag what they are told to tag (NFL injury video review operators; rugby union video support technicians). The course teaches the third tier as the job and the first two as the people that job serves.
